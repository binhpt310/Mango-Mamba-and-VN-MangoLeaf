1. Introduction
    Mango (Mangifera indica L.) cultivation is a vital component of the global agricultural economy, yet it faces
substantial production risks due to foliar pathogens. Diseases such as Anthracnose, Powdery Mildew, and Red Rust
can cause yield losses exceeding 20-30% if not managed effectively [1]. Traditional disease management relies on
manual visual inspection by pathologists, a process that is labor-intensive, subjective, and difficult to scale in large
orchards or resource-limited regions [9]. Consequently, the development of automated, accurate, and real-time disease
detection systems has become a priority in smart agriculture.
    Recent advancements in deep learning have significantly improved automated plant disease diagnosis. Convolu-
tional Neural Networks (CNNs) [14, 8] and Transformer-based architectures [6, 2] have achieved high classification
accuracy on benchmark datasets. However, the practical deployment of these models on edge devices (e.g., smart-
phones, embedded sensors) is hindered by computational constraints. While Vision Transformers (ViTs) offer superior
global context modeling compared to CNNs, they suffer from a quadratic computational complexity (𝑂(𝑁 2 )) with
respect to image resolution due to the self-attention mechanism [7]. This results in high memory consumption and
latency, rendering them unsuitable for real-time applications in rural environments where internet connectivity for
cloud processing is often unreliable [9].
    Additionally, recent studies have demonstrated the potential of advanced hybrid architectures in addressing
performance and computational challenges. For instance, Qin et al. [11] introduced CropCapsNet, which integrates an
enhanced capsule network with Squeeze-and-Excitation Inception modules. This approach effectively captures multi-
scale features for crop disease classification, achieving remarkable accuracy by overcoming the spatial information
loss inherent in traditional CNNs. Similarly, Truong et al. [17] proposed SC-MambaFew, utilizing the Mamba
architecture coupled with selective spatial-channel attention for bearing fault diagnosis. Their work highlights the
capability of Mamba-based hybrids to model long-range dependencies efficiently, particularly in few-shot learning

leaf diseases on strictly resource-constrained edge hardware. Furthermore, the issue of cross-domain generalization
against geographical bias- a critical requirement in global agriculture-remains largely unaddressed in these architectural

     To address the limitations of quadratic complexity while ensuring high diagnostic precision, State Space Models
(SSMs), particularly the Mamba architecture [3], have emerged as a promising alternative. Mamba introduces a
selective scan mechanism that allows for modeling long-range dependencies with linear computational complexity
(𝑂(𝑁)). Recent adaptations of Mamba for computer vision, such as MambaVision [5] and Vision Mamba [22], have
demonstrated that SSMs can rival Transformers in performance while significantly reducing inference costs. Despite
these advances, the specific optimization of hybrid Mamba-Attention architectures for lightweight agricultural disease
classification remains underexplored. Existing lightweight models often sacrifice accuracy for speed or fail to capture
the multi-scale features required to distinguish between morphologically similar mango leaf diseases [21].
     Furthermore, a critical gap exists in the geographical diversity of available mango leaf disease datasets. The
majority of public datasets, including MangoLeafBD [1] and MLD24 [15], originate primarily from Bangladesh. This
geographical bias limits the generalization capability of models when applied to regions with different mango cultivars
and disease prevalence, such as Vietnam, where distinct pathogens like Red Rust are more common [10].
     To bridge these gaps, this study proposes MangoMamba, a lightweight architecture specifically engineered for
efficient mango leaf disease classification on edge devices. The architecture integrates Multi-Scale Mamba Mixers
for linear-complexity global context modeling with Large-Kernel Attention (LKA) mechanisms for local feature
refinement. Additionally, the VN-MangoLeaf dataset is introduced to address regional data scarcity, complemented
by a curriculum learning strategy to enhance cross-domain generalization.
     The primary contributions of this research are summarized as follows:
   1. A specialized hybrid architecture, MangoMamba, is developed, combining the efficiency of Selective State
      Space Models with the spatial precision of Large-Kernel Attention. This design achieves linear computational
      complexity, significantly reducing inference latency compared to Transformer-based counterparts.
   2. The VN-MangoLeaf dataset is introduced, comprising 7,000 images collected from Vietnamese orchards. This
      dataset mitigates geographical bias by including regionally specific classes such as Red Rust, enabling more
      robust model evaluation.
   3. A three-phase curriculum learning methodology is implemented to facilitate effective knowledge transfer
      between datasets with varying class distributions, enhancing the model’s robustness against morphological
      variations.
   4. Extensive experimental validation demonstrates that the proposed model achieves competitive accuracy (99.75%
      on MangoLeafBD and 98.14% on VN-MangoLeaf) while maintaining an inference time of approximately 1.5
      ms per image, verifying its suitability for real-time mobile deployment.


2. Materials and Methods
Datasets overview
    Existing public datasets for mango leaf disease detection, such as MangoLeafBD [1] and MLD24 [15], have
provided valuable benchmarks for the research community. However, these datasets originate predominantly from
Bangladesh, which limits the generalization capability of models when applied to other geographical regions with
different environmental conditions and pathogen distributions. To address this geographical bias and the lack of diverse
regional data, this study introduces the VN-MangoLeaf dataset, collected extensively across southern Vietnam.
    A critical distinction of the VN-MangoLeaf dataset is the specific disease distribution observed in Vietnam
compared to Bangladesh (Figure 1). While diseases such as ‘Cutting Weevil’ and ‘Bacterial Canker’ are common
in previous datasets, they are infrequently observed in Vietnamese mango orchards. Conversely, ‘Red Rust’, caused by
the algal plant pathogen Cephaleuros virescens [10], is highly prevalent in this region but underrepresented in existing
benchmarks. Red Rust manifests as orange-brown, rust-like spots, typically on the upper leaf surface [10], as illustrated
in Figure 2.
    The dataset comprises seven distinct classes: Healthy, Anthracnose, Gall Midge, Powdery Mildew, Sooty Mold,
Dieback, and Red Rust. Images were acquired using a Xiaomi Redmi Note 11S smartphone camera. To ensure high

2.2. MangoMamba model’s architecture
   The proposed MangoMamba architecture is a lightweight, hierarchical vision backbone specifically engineered for
mango leaf disease recognition on edge devices. It prioritizes a compact model size and rapid inference capabilities

while maintaining high classification accuracy. The architecture adopts a four-stage structure with progressively
decreasing spatial resolutions (𝐻∕4 → 𝐻∕32) and increasing channel dimensions (𝐶 → 8𝐶). To maintain linear
computational complexity, each stage processes features through non-overlapping windows.
    Unlike standard hybrid architectures that combine State Space Models (SSM) with computationally intensive
Transformer blocks, MangoMamba introduces a hybrid integration of Mamba Mixers and Large-Kernel Attention
(LKA). This design strategically utilizes the global receptive field of SSMs for sequence modeling and the spatial
adaptability of LKA for capturing local disease textures, effectively eliminating the quadratic complexity associated
with traditional Self-Attention.
    The model integrates several advanced components, selected based on extensive ablation studies to maximize the
trade-off between efficiency and performance:

    • Global Response Normalization (GRN): Utilized to enhance the contrast and selectivity of channels, thereby
      preventing feature collapse and stabilizing training dynamics. This component, adapted from ConvNeXt V2
      [20], ensures that feature diversity is maintained across the deep layers of the network.
    • Efficient Channel Attention (ECA): Implemented to provide channel-wise feature enhancement with negligible
      parameter overhead [19]. By emphasizing informative channels, ECA allows the model to focus on distinct
      disease characteristics amidst complex leaf backgrounds.
    • Windowed Processing: Inspired by the hierarchical design of the Swin Transformer [7], feature maps are
      partitioned into local windows. This approach facilitates efficient local attention computation and hierarchical
      feature representation, eliminating the heavy computational burden associated with global attention.
    • Multi-Scale Mamba Mixers: Inspired by the MambaVision architecture [5], this component is adapted to model
      multi-resolution features. It preserves the linear scaling superiority of SSMs [3], enabling the model to effectively
      capture long-range dependencies across the leaf surface.
    • Large-Kernel Attention (LKA): Incorporated to replace the Transformer component found in typical hybrid
      models. LKA decomposes large kernel convolutions to capture spatial correlations and long-range dependencies
      with lower computational cost and parameter count compared to standard self-attention mechanisms [4].
    • SwiGLU Activation: Employed within the Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) blocks, SwiGLU offers enhanced
      representational capacity compared to standard GELU or ReLU activations [16]. It operates as a gated linear
      unit with a sigmoid-activated component, improving the model’s ability to learn complex non-linear disease
      patterns.

    As visualized in Figure 6, the input processing commences with a lightweight patch embedding module. This
module efficiently transforms raw images into patch-level representations using two consecutive stride-2 convolutions,
reducing the spatial dimension to a quarter of the input resolution while projecting to the base channel dimension.
    To ensure robust feature initialization, this embedding is immediately refined by the ECA and GRN mechanisms.
The ECA module generates channel-wise attention weights by applying a parameter-efficient 1D convolution over
global average pooling features, followed by a sigmoid activation. These weights are then element-wise multiplied with
the input features to recalibrate channel importance. Subsequently, the GRN mechanism stabilizes feature responses
by normalizing the spatial L2-norm across height and width dimensions, followed by a learnable affine transformation.
This process emphasizes spatially important regions and suppresses redundant information.
    The core of the architecture employs a hierarchical four-stage design utilizing windowed processing. In each stage,
feature maps are partitioned into non-overlapping windows of size 𝑆 × 𝑆. This partitioning transforms the input tensor

    Within the processing stages, the architecture utilizes a specific intra-stage hybrid strategy. The initial blocks of
each stage employ Mamba Mixers to scan the windowed sequences and establish global context, while the subsequent

the global structure of the leaf and lesion distribution before focusing on the fine-grained visual patterns of the disease.
Between stages, depthwise separable convolutions with a stride of 2 are employed for efficient downsampling, followed
by additional ECA and GRN modules to preserve feature distinctiveness during resolution reduction.

2.3. MangoMambaBlock
    The MangoMambaBlock serves as the foundational computational unit within each stage of the architecture,
designed to efficiently extract and refine features for mango leaf disease classification. As illustrated in Figure 7, this
block utilizes a dual-pathway residual structure that integrates advanced mixing mechanisms with a robust feed-forward
network. The design is engineered to balance the capture of local textural details-essential for identifying specific
lesions like Anthracnose-with global context, which is required to detect broader stress symptoms such as Dieback.
    To achieve this balance while minimizing computational overhead, the architecture employs an intra-stage hybrid
strategy. The initial blocks of a stage utilize the Multi-Scale Mamba Mixer to model long-range dependencies and
global sequence patterns with linear complexity. Conversely, the latter blocks of the stage switch to Large-Kernel
Attention (LKA) to refine explicit spatial attention and local correlations. This alternation is determined by the block’s
depth index; empirically, activating LKA in the second half of the stage enhances the model’s ability to discriminate
between visually similar disease classes by focusing on fine-grained spatial textures after the global context has been
established.
    The signal propagation within the MangoMambaBlock follows a standard pre-normalization residual design. The
input token sequence first undergoes Layer Normalization before entering the designated Mixer module (either Mamba
or LKA). The output of the mixer is scaled by a learnable layer-scaling coefficient and subjected to stochastic depth
(DropPath) regularization before being added back to the original input. This process is repeated for the second half of
the block, which consists of a Layer Normalization step followed by a SwiGLU-based Feed-Forward Network (FFN).
The inclusion of learnable layer scaling is critical for stabilizing the training of deep networks by mitigating potential
gradient distinctness issues.

    To address the challenge of scale variance in disease symptoms, the Multi-Scale Mamba Mixer processes windowed
tokens through two parallel branches. A high-resolution branch processes the native token sequence to preserve fine
details, while a low-resolution branch operates on a spatially downsampled version to capture broader semantic features.
The low-resolution input is generated via bilinear interpolation, processed, and then upsampled back to the original
size. Both branches utilize the Disease Aware Mixer (DAM), and their outputs are fused via a learnable, channel-
wise sigmoid gate. This gating mechanism allows the network to adaptively weigh the importance of fine versus
coarse features for each channel, optimizing the representation without the parameter explosion typical of dense fusion
methods.
    The DAM module is a lightweight adaptation of the mixer found in MambaVision [5], specifically optimized for
disease pattern recognition. It reduces the state dimension and convolution kernel size to prioritize inference speed on
edge devices while preserving the selective reasoning capabilities of State Space Models (SSM). Internally, the input
projection is split into two streams. The first stream undergoes a 1D depthwise convolution to capture local context,
followed by a selective state-space operation where the discretized state-transition parameters are derived dynamically
from the input. This enables the model to selectively propagate or forget information along the sequence, effectively
modeling the interconnected nature of disease symptoms across leaf veins. The output is then gated by the second
stream (the z-stream) to regulate information flow before the final output projection.
    In the later layers of the stage, the block utilizes Large-Kernel Attention to generate spatial attention maps. Unlike
the Mamba mixer, LKA is designed to capture spatial correlations directly without the quadratic cost of self-attention.
It decomposes a large kernel convolution into a sequence of smaller operations: a standard depthwise convolution
to capture local dependencies, followed by a dilated depthwise convolution to expand the receptive field. In this

    To enhance the non-linear representational capacity of the block, a Position-wise Feed-Forward Network with
SwiGLU activation is employed. Selected based on ablation results demonstrating its superiority over standard GELU,
the SwiGLU mechanism projects the input into two parallel linear layers. One path is activated by the SiLU function
and then element-wise multiplied by the output of the second path. This gating interaction allows the network to learn
more complex feature transformations, contributing to the high classification accuracy observed in the experiments.

2.4. Proposed methodology
    To address data scarcity and adapt to the specific label-set variations across different agricultural datasets, a three-
phase curriculum learning strategy is implemented. This approach, visualized in Figure 8, progressively broadens the
data diversity before narrowing down to the specific target domain.

    • Phase 1: Source Domain Initialization (MangoLeafBD). The MangoMamba backbone is initialized with
      an 8-class linear projection head. Training is conducted end-to-end on the MangoLeafBD dataset. This phase
      establishes a foundational representation of canonical mango lesion features, creating a baseline checkpoint.
    • Phase 2: Robust Feature Learning (Combined Dataset). The checkpoint from Phase 1 is loaded and fine-tuned
      on a composite dataset constructed from MangoLeafBD, the Mango Leaf Disease Dataset, and MLD24. This
      phase exposes the model to significant variations in illumination, capture devices, and lesion morphology while
      maintaining the original 8-way classification scheme. Lower layers are optionally frozen during the initial epochs

      to preserve low-level feature extractors, followed by full-model adaptation. This step is critical for learning
      invariance to environmental conditions.
    • Phase 3: Target Domain Adaptation (VN-MangoLeaf). In the final phase, the linear classifier is replaced to
      accommodate the 7-class label set of the target VN-MangoLeaf dataset. The backbone retains weights from the
      Combined checkpoint. Since the target dataset excludes classes such as ’Cutting Weevil’ and ’Bacterial Canker’
      but emphasizes ’Red Rust’, the adaptation process biases the updates toward high-level semantic features while
      retaining the low-level textural invariances learned in Phase 2. This strategy effectively manages the domain
      shift and class imbalance inherent in the target environment.

3. Results and Discussion
3.1. Ablation study
    To validate the effectiveness of the proposed MangoMamba architecture and justify the inclusion of each specific
component, a comprehensive ablation study was conducted on the MangoLeafBD dataset. The experiments were
performed using consistent hyperparameters across four independent runs for each configuration to ensure statistical
reliability. The results, summarizing the impact of the Mixer core, enhancement modules (ECA, GRN, SwiGLU), and
windowing strategies, are presented in Table 4.
    The core hypothesis of MangoMamba is that combining State Space Models with Large-Kernel Attention offers
a balanced trade-off between global context modeling and parameter efficiency compared to pure architectures. As
shown in Table 4, the Pure LKA model (Case 1) is the most lightweight (3.01M parameters) but lacks the sequence
modeling capabilities required for complex disease patterns, resulting in lower stability. Conversely, the Pure Mamba
model (Case 4) achieves competitive accuracy (99.25%) but incurs a significant parameter penalty (6.62M), which
is less ideal for edge deployment. The Hybrid architecture without additional features (Case 3) strikes a balance,
achieving 99.12% accuracy with 4.41M parameters. This confirms that integrating Mamba Mixers in early layers to
capture global dependencies and LKA in later layers for spatial refinement provides an optimal foundation for the
model.
    The necessity of the integrated components—Efficient Channel Attention (ECA), Global Response Normalization
(GRN), and SwiGLU—was evaluated by selectively removing them from the hybrid baseline.

      • Impact of ECA (Case 6): Removing the ECA module resulted in a significant increase in variance (±1.52%) and
        a drop in accuracy to 98.56%. This indicates that the channel-wise attention mechanism is critical for stabilizing
        feature selection and focusing the model on relevant disease symptoms amidst background noise.

      • Impact of GRN (Case 7): While removing GRN maintained high accuracy (99.50%), the inclusion of GRN
        in the final configuration (Case 8) contributed to a higher F1-score (99.69% vs. 99.62%). GRN is retained to
        prevent feature collapse in deep layers and ensure the model prioritizes high-contrast features, which is essential
        for generalization across diverse datasets.
      • Impact of SwiGLU (Case 5): Replacing SwiGLU with standard activations resulted in a slight decrease in F1-
        score compared to the optimal configuration. The SwiGLU activation enhances the non-linear representational
        capacity of the MLP blocks, allowing the model to capture more intricate decision boundaries between similar
        disease classes.

    A critical finding from the ablation study is the impact of window size configuration. The initial design utilized a
multi-scale window strategy ([8, 8, 14, 7]) to mimic hierarchical receptive fields (Case 9). However, empirical results
demonstrate that a Fixed Window strategy of [7, 7, 7, 7] (Case 8) yields better performance, achieving the highest

7 × 7 aligns better with the typical spatial extent of mango leaf lesions at the processed feature resolutions, minimizing
boundary artifacts often introduced by varying window sizes. Consequently, the Fixed Window configuration (Case 8)
was selected as the final architecture for MangoMamba.

3.2. Training setup
    The experimental validation of the MangoMamba model was conducted within a standardized computational
environment to ensure reproducibility and accessibility for resource-constrained agricultural applications. All training
and evaluation procedures were executed on the Google Colab platform (Free Tier), utilizing a single NVIDIA Tesla
T4 GPU (15 GB VRAM) and 12.7 GB of system RAM. The software stack was built upon Python 3.12 and PyTorch
2.6 with CUDA 12.6 support. Key dependencies included NumPy 2.2.2, Matplotlib 3.10.3, and Scikit-learn 1.7.1 for
data handling and analysis; Transformers 4.50, TIMM 1.0.9, and Einops 0.8.1 for model architecture components; and
Mamba-SSM 2.2.4 for the state-space modeling operations.
    As delineated in Section 2.1, the datasets were stratified into distinct subsets: 80% for training, 10% for validation,
and 10% for testing. To ensure assessment of generalization capability, all performance metrics reported in subsequent
sections are derived exclusively from the held-out test set, which comprises images completely unseen during the
optimization process.
    The training configuration was standardized across all experiments to maintain consistency. The specific hyper-
parameters are detailed in Table 5. The model was optimized using the AdamW optimizer with a cosine annealing
learning rate scheduler, initialized at a learning rate of 5 × 10−4 following a 5-epoch linear warmup period. This
warmup strategy is critical for stabilizing the early training dynamics of the hybrid Mamba-LKA architecture.
    Despite the substantial volume of training data, a comprehensive suite of regularization techniques was employed
to mitigate overfitting and simulate the noise characteristics of real-world deployment. Label smoothing was applied to
prevent the model from becoming over-confident in its predictions, thereby encouraging softer probability distributions.
Weight decay was utilized to penalize large weights and promote parameter sparsity. Stochastic depth (DropPath) was
implemented to randomly drop residual paths during training, effectively training an implicit ensemble of sub-networks.
Additionally, gradient clipping was enforced to stabilize the optimization of the recurrent state-space components, and
an early stopping mechanism with a patience of 20 epochs was used to halt training when validation performance
plateaued. These measures collectively ensure that the final deployed model maintains robust performance on edge
devices.

3.7. Error analysis
    While the MangoMamba model achieves high overall classification accuracy, a granular analysis of misclassified
samples reveals specific limitations in distinguishing phenotypically similar diseases. Figure 13 illustrates represen-
tative error cases accompanied by Grad-CAM visualizations, which serve to diagnose whether failures stem from
incorrect spatial localization or feature ambiguity.
    Two primary misclassification patterns are observed. The first involves confusion between Anthracnose and Red
Rust (Rows 1, 2, and 4 in Figure 13). In these instances, the model incorrectly predicts the class with moderate
confidence (e.g., 0.511-0.665). The Grad-CAM heatmaps demonstrate that the attention mechanism correctly activates
on the necrotic lesions, indicating that the model successfully localizes the pathology. However, the decision boundary
remains ambiguous due to the shared reddish-brown chromatic characteristics of both diseases. This suggests that the
feature extractor prioritizes dominant color features over the subtle textural differences (e.g., the powdery pustules of
Red Rust versus the sunken necrosis of Anthracnose).
    The second pattern involves the misclassification of Gall Midge as Sooty Mold (Row 3). Both conditions manifest
as dark, clustered masses on the leaf surface. Although the model accurately focuses on the gall structures, the
morphological similarity in low-contrast regions leads to erroneous predictions.
    These findings imply that while the hybrid Mamba-Attention architecture excels at capturing global context and
localizing symptoms (as evidenced by precise Grad-CAM activation), it occasionally struggles with fine-grained
texture discrimination when inter-class color similarity is high. The relatively low confidence scores in these
misclassified cases suggest that integrating an uncertainty threshold mechanism could effectively flag such ambiguous
samples for expert review in practical deployment.

3.8. Limitations and Future Research Directions
    While the experimental results demonstrate that the proposed MangoMamba architecture achieves a favorable
trade-off between computational efficiency and classification accuracy, several limitations regarding the study’s scope
and deployment validity are acknowledged.
    First, regarding the hardware deployment claims, although the model was designed for edge devices, the current
empirical validation of real-time inference is limited to a specific mid-range smartphone configuration (Xiaomi Redmi
Note 11S). While this device confirms the feasibility of the architecture with an inference latency suitable for interactive
use, the performance on lower-end devices or different mobile chipsets (e.g., older Snapdragon series or MediaTek
entry-level chips) has not been comprehensively benchmarked. Furthermore, the current deployment relies on the raw
PyTorch mobile runtime. The potential gains from hardware-specific optimizations, such as quantization (INT8) or
conversion to ONNX/TensorFlow Lite formats, which are critical for broad cross-platform compatibility, remain an
open area for investigation.

    Second, the generalization capability of the VN-MangoLeaf dataset presents geographical constraints. Although the
dataset successfully addresses the bias of existing Bangladesh-centric repositories by introducing Vietnamese-specific
disease variants like Red Rust, the data collection was primarily concentrated in Southern Vietnam. Consequently, the
model may exhibit reduced sensitivity to disease manifestations specific to Northern Vietnam or other tropical regions
with differing climatic conditions. Additionally, the current taxonomy includes seven distinct classes; however, other
economically significant mango pathologies, such as Bacterial Black Spot (Xanthomonas campestris) or Malformation
disease, are absent from the training distribution. This limitation implies that the system classifies unseen pathologies
as one of the existing seven categories rather than identifying them as "unknown" or "out-of-distribution."
    Third, while data augmentation and curriculum learning were employed to mitigate environmental variability, the
robustness of the model under extreme field conditions—such as varying sunlight angles at dusk/dawn, severe occlusion

by overlapping foliage, or motion blur caused by wind—requires further rigorous field testing. The current evaluation
relies heavily on static images, whereas real-world agricultural diagnosis often involves dynamic video streams.
    Future research directions will focus on three key objectives to address these shortcomings.
    • Cross-Platform ptimization: Future work will implement and evaluate model quantization and pruning
      techniques to establish a comprehensive benchmark across a diverse fleet of Android and iOS devices, ensuring
      consistent real-time performance regardless of hardware tier.
    • Dataset expansion: The VN-MangoLeaf dataset will be expanded through a citizen science approach, incor-
      porating samples from diverse geographical latitudes to capture broader symptom variations. Concurrently, the
      architecture will be adapted to support open-set recognition, enabling the model to flag unknown diseases rather
      than forcing a misclassification.
    • Integrated pest management system: Beyond singular leaf disease classification, future iterations aim to
      integrate the MangoMamba backbone into a multi-modal system capable of analyzing fruit quality and pest
      infestation simultaneously, providing a holistic diagnostic tool for precision agriculture.

4. Conclusion
    This study introduced MangoMamba, a lightweight hybrid architecture that effectively balances classification
accuracy with computational efficiency for mango leaf disease detection. By integrating Multi-Scale Mamba Mixers
with Large-Kernel Attention, the proposed model achieved competitive accuracies of 99.75% on the MangoLeafBD
dataset and 98.71% on the newly curated VN-MangoLeaf dataset (7,000 images). With a compact footprint of 5.8
million parameters and a file size of 22.2 MB, the model demonstrates linear complexity, enabling an inference
speed of 1.46 ms per image on GPU, representing an approximate 80× speedup compared to recent Transformer-
based approaches like ViX-MangoEFormer. Crucially, practical deployment feasibility was validated via an ONNX-
based Android application, achieving stable inference latencies of 100-300 ms on mobile devices without internet
dependency. Ablation studies confirmed that the synergistic combination of Mamba mixers and attention mechanisms
significantly minimizes feature redundancy while maximizing diagnostic precision. Future work will focus on integrat-
ing uncertainty quantification to handle ambiguous cases and expanding the dataset to include diverse environmental
conditions for broader agro-ecological generalization.
